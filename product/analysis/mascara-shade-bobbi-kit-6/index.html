Mascara shade strategy for Bobbi Kit 6.0

The kit is currently planned with a brown mascara only. This brief tests whether that shade choice serves our whole customer base — and recommends a fix.

Prepared for PayalJune 2026Question raised in kit review
The Recommendation

Offer both black and brown in the kit — with black as the default. If the kit can only hold one fixed mascara, make it black, not brown.

A brown-only kit is the single weakest option, both for inclusivity and for raw conversion. The reason isn't taste — it's mechanical: brown mascara barely shows up on dark lashes, which describes most Black, Asian, and Latina customers. Black works on everyone. Confidence: high on direction, moderate on exact magnitude.

Why The Shade Matters

Brown is a light-features product. Black is for everyone.

Every independent source — L'Oréal, No7, Thrive, Shine, The Zoe Report — lands in the same place on who each shade is built for. Brown reads as "soft and natural" precisely because it's close in tone to light lashes. On already-dark lashes it delivers almost no contrast, so it disappears and defeats the purpose of mascara.

Brown mascara

Fair skin · blonde, red or light-brown hair · light eyes

Recommended as a softer, low-contrast enhancement for lighter features. Even among blondes — its core audience — sentiment leans slightly back toward black.

Black mascara

Medium-to-deep skin · dark hair · dark eyes

The recommended shade for deeper complexions and dark lashes — it "complements the depth of their features" and is the only shade that delivers visible definition.

The customers a brown-only kit serves worst are exactly the ones we'd least want to underserve.

The Demographic Skew

This lands hardest on our most makeup-engaged customers

  • Hispanic / Latina: the most makeup-engaged segment in the US — over-index on mascara use by 20%+ and spend ~19% more on beauty than non-Hispanic white women, with an aesthetic that leans toward dramatic, defined, dark lashes.
  • Black customers: dark lashes against a deeper complexion — black is the definition shade; brown tends to vanish.
  • East-Asian customers: predominantly very dark, often sparse lashes where visible contrast matters most — black is near-universal; brown frequently reads as invisible.

Note on confidence: clean per-ethnicity black-vs-brown survey splits don't really exist publicly, so the ethnic read rests on the universal expert guidance plus the contrast mechanism plus the Latina usage data — strong on direction, lighter on a precise number.

The Brand Risk & Precedent

No brown-mascara scandal exists — but the pattern that burns brands does

There's no documented case of a brand being dragged for a brown-only mascara specifically; mascara shade rarely goes viral the way foundation does. But "a shade range tuned to light features" is one of the most reliable ways beauty brands have gotten into trouble, and the playbook is always the same.

Tarte — Shape Tape Foundation (2018)

Launched 15 shades, only 3 for deep skin. Immediate backlash, influencers pressured not to endorse, public apology within five days, forced relaunch as Face Tape with 50 shades.

Youthforia — Date Night Foundation (2023–24)

Launched with too few deep shades; the eventual "darkest" shade drew a 24M-view TikTok comparison to black face paint and CNN coverage, forcing a statement and a line extension.

The structural analog — "default to light = nude"

Ballet pointe shoes, "flesh-tone" bandages and "nude" tights were all historically made in one pale shade — and all eventually hit petitions and public reckonings. A kit shipping one shade tuned to light features is the same shape of problem.

Our risk is quieter than a viral pile-on but more certain: a functional miss (the mascara underperforms on dark lashes → weaker reviews and returns from those buyers) and a brand-signal miss for a company whose own line "celebrates the individual beauty of all women." In every case above, the brand didn't intend exclusion — it just optimized for the light-featured default and got read as exclusionary after the fact.

What To Do

The asymmetry is the whole argument

  • Best: include both shades, default black, brown as the swap. Mascara is a low-cost SKU against kit AOV — cheap insurance.
  • If one fixed shade only: make it black. Black is universal; brown is the exclusionary choice and is minority-preferred even among white consumers.
  • Avoid: brown-only. Saves nothing, quietly underserves Black, Asian and Latina customers, and is off-brand against Bobbi's own black-mascara heritage.
