Black vs. brown mascara: preference across demographics

A summary of what consumers, experts, and market data say about black vs. brown mascara preference across skin tones and ethnicities — prompted by the question of whether Bobbi Kit 6.0 should carry a brown mascara only.

Prepared for PayalJune 2026Web research, hand-verified
What the research found
  • Black is the dominant mascara shade in the US — about 62% preference vs. 21% brown and 17% colored, roughly a 3:1 lead for black even before segmenting by ethnicity.
  • Every expert and brand source agrees on who each shade suits: brown is positioned for fair skin and blonde/red/light-brown hair; black is recommended for medium-to-deep skin, dark hair, and dark eyes.
  • The driver is contrast. Brown reads as "soft and natural" because it's close in tone to light lashes — and on already-dark lashes it delivers little visible definition.
  • Black, Asian, and Latina consumers predominantly have dark lashes. Latinas in particular over-index on mascara use (+20% vs. non-Hispanic white women) and lean toward dramatic, defined lashes.
  • Brown is a real, growing trend — but it skews toward lighter-featured consumers and the "soft, effortless" look, not darker features.
  • Brands that shipped shade ranges tuned to light features (Tarte, Youthforia) faced public backlash and forced expansions — a recurring pattern, though never specifically over mascara.

Expert & Brand Guidance

Brown is positioned as a light-features shade; black as the universal one

Independent sources — L'Oréal, No7, Thrive, Shine, The Zoe Report — land in the same place on who each shade is built for. The consistent explanation is contrast: brown enhances light lashes softly, but fades on dark ones.

Brown mascara

Fair skin · blonde, red or light-brown hair · light eyes

Recommended as a softer, low-contrast enhancement for lighter features. Even among blondes — its core audience — consumer sentiment still leans slightly back toward black.

Black mascara

Medium-to-deep skin · dark hair · dark eyes

The recommended shade for deeper complexions and dark lashes — sources say it "complements the depth of their features" and delivers the visible definition brown can't.

"Black suits everyone but can be harsh on light features; brown enhances light eyes and hair beautifully" — the recurring expert framing.

Demographic Signal

The dark-lash majority skews to black

  • Hispanic / Latina: the most makeup-engaged segment in the US — over-index on mascara use by 20%+ and spend ~19% more on beauty than non-Hispanic white women, with an aesthetic that leans toward dramatic, defined, dark lashes.
  • Black consumers: dark lashes against a deeper complexion — black is the definition shade; brown tends to read as faint or invisible.
  • East-Asian consumers: predominantly very dark, often sparse lashes where visible contrast matters most — black is near-universal; brown frequently disappears.
  • Fair / lighter-featured consumers: the one group where brown genuinely competes — and even here, preference splits, with many still choosing black.

Confidence note: clean per-ethnicity black-vs-brown survey splits don't exist publicly, so the ethnic read rests on the universal expert guidance plus the contrast mechanism plus the Latina usage data — strong on direction, lighter on a precise number.

Industry Precedent

How analogous shade decisions have played out

There's no documented case of a brand being criticized for a brown-only mascara specifically — mascara shade rarely goes viral the way foundation does. But "a shade range tuned to light features" is a recurring source of backlash, and the pattern is consistent.

Tarte — Shape Tape Foundation (2018)

Launched 15 shades, only 3 for deep skin. Immediate backlash, influencers pressured not to endorse, public apology within five days, forced relaunch as Face Tape with 50 shades.

Youthforia — Date Night Foundation (2023–24)

Launched with too few deep shades; the eventual "darkest" shade drew a 24M-view TikTok comparison to black face paint and CNN coverage, forcing a statement and a line extension.

The structural analog — "default to light = nude"

Ballet pointe shoes, "flesh-tone" bandages and "nude" tights were all historically made in one pale shade — and all eventually hit petitions and public reckonings over the same light-default assumption.

If You Want A Single Takeaway

The findings point one direction: offering both black and brown (with black as the default) serves the whole base; if the kit can only carry one fixed shade, black is the safer choice because it works across all skin tones, while brown-only underserves the dark-lashed majority. Confidence is high on direction, moderate on exact magnitude.
